Project Problem Framing
Reflow | Bernardo Moreira
1. Problem
Amateur athletes trying to juggle multiple sports spend two scarce resources trying to reach their goals: money and time.
They spend money on different training apps, AI plans, and coaches, while also looking for advice through YouTube videos and communities. Even with all these resources, they still have to figure out how everything fits together. A running plan might help with running, but the athlete still needs to decide how to fit soccer, swimming, or strength training around it.
This also takes time. When they miss a workout or their schedule changes, they have to bring together information from different sources and decide what to adjust. They want to enjoy their sports, maintain a consistent routine, and reach their goals, but coordinating everything becomes another task.
This idea comes from my own experience practicing several sports and working with multiple coaches and a nutritionist. I have had to figure out how their different recommendations fit into one routine. That experience gives me a starting point, but I still need to talk to other athletes to understand how often they face this problem and whether they would use a product to solve it.
2. Primary User
My primary user is an adult amateur athlete who practices at least one recreational sport, one endurance sport such as running, cycling, or swimming, and some form of strength training. They have specific fitness or performance goals, but they also want to have fun and fit training around their life. Their main difficulty is understanding how to balance everything, especially when their week changes.
Today, they combine different apps, coaches, and online advice, or make adjustments themselves. They might adopt Reflow because it gives them one place to discuss their whole routine, understand what to change, and get guidance when they need it. For the first prototype, I will use running, soccer, and strength training as the example.
3. Proposed Product Direction
I am proposing an AI fitness coach that users can talk to about their training across different sports. It should ask relevant questions, explain its recommendations, and push back when a request does not fit the user’s goals or available time.
The user would enter their goal, weekly plan, availability, and recent workouts. They could then ask how to adjust after something changes.
For example, someone might have five runs planned but skip one to play soccer. They could log the game’s duration and perceived intensity and ask what that means for the rest of their week. The coach would ask for any missing information and explain possible adjustments. It should not automatically assume that soccer replaces the run or that the user needs to add those missed miles elsewhere.
The user could ask follow-up questions and accept, edit, or reject the proposed changes. The main value is having a coach that considers the whole routine and explains how to adjust, instead of simply following every request. The first prototype will use text chat, with live conversations remaining part of the longer-term idea.
4. AI-Fit Rationale
What AI will do: AI will handle the conversation, identify relevant information, ask follow-up questions, and recommend an adjustment with an explanation.
Inputs: It will receive the user’s goals, weekly plan, availability, recent activities, duration, perceived intensity, and question. I also plan to provide a small set of reviewed training-reference summaries to support its responses.
These inputs could reveal personal routines, and users might leave out information or log it incorrectly. For class testing, I will use fictional profiles without identifying information or medical records. Users will enter their own workout summaries rather than upload copyrighted plans. I will avoid permanent storage in the prototype and check the AI provider’s data-retention settings before testing with real information.
Outputs and user control: The coach will return questions, a suggested adjustment, and an explanation of the trade-offs and uncertainty. Users will confirm that it understood them correctly and review the changes before accepting anything.
Why AI fits: Users will describe different situations in their own words and need to discuss their options. AI can make that conversation more flexible than a fixed form or a set of prepared answers. Rules can still check whether a proposed schedule fits the user’s availability and limits. I will compare this approach with a simpler rule-based version to see whether AI adds enough value.
Risks and safeguards: AI could misunderstand an activity, give incorrect advice, overlook a goal, favor one sport, or sound more certain than it should. The coach will show its assumptions, ask for missing information, and use the supplied references without inventing sources. Users will review all changes, and the system will check scheduling constraints. Medical, injury, and rehabilitation questions will be outside the prototype’s scope. If the system cannot support a recommendation, it should say so. If the AI service fails, the original plan will remain available.
The AI inside Reflow is separate from using ChatGPT or Replit to help me develop the product.
5. Non-Goals and Prototype Boundaries
The first version will focus on one user’s weekly plan and one coaching conversation. I am not trying to build the full application yet.
It will not include payment processing, multiple account roles, or a coach dashboard. Live video or voice coaching, connections to wearables or other training apps, and a native mobile application are also outside the first version. It will not build complete training plans from scratch, provide nutrition, medical, injury, or rehabilitation advice, or make automatic changes without the user’s approval.
I am also not aiming for production-level security or scalability at this stage, and I will not claim that the coaching has been medically validated.
6. Preparation for the First Working Slice
In Replit, I will build a page where a user can enter or load a sample training week, log a missed or substituted workout, and ask the coach how to adjust.
The system will confirm the situation, ask for missing details, and show a proposed adjustment with an explanation. The user will be able to accept it and update the displayed week, or reject it and keep the original.
The flow will be successful if the user can complete that conversation and receive a proposal that respects their stated availability and limits. If there is no supported adjustment, the system should explain the problem rather than force an answer.
I will test three situations. First, a missed run when another training slot is available. The coach should suggest an adjustment, explain it, and let the user accept or reject it. Second, a soccer game instead of a run when duration or intensity is missing. The coach should ask for more information and avoid assuming that the activities are equivalent. Third, a user who wants to make up every missed session but does not have enough time. The coach should push back, explain the conflict, and offer a choice.
I will record whether the flow works, whether recommendations respect the constraints, and whether testers find the explanations useful. Known limitations will include self-reported information, a small reference set, inconsistent AI responses, and no evidence yet that the product improves fitness outcomes.
